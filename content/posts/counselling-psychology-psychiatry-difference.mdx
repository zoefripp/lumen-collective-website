When you decide you want to talk to someone, the next question is often "who?". Counsellor, psychologist, psychiatrist: the titles sound similar and are often used as if they mean the same thing. They do not. Each has a different background, a different focus and a different cost.

Here is a plain-English guide to help you work out where to start.

## Counsellors

Counsellors are trained to help people talk through what is going on, understand their thoughts and feelings, and find ways forward. Training varies, from diplomas to postgraduate degrees, and many counsellors bring years of experience from related fields such as community work, youth work or disability support.

Counselling is a good fit for things like:

- stress, worry and feeling overwhelmed
- big life changes, such as a relationship ending, a move or a new diagnosis
- grief and loss
- relationship and family difficulties
- feeling stuck, low or unsure of what you want
- carer stress and burnout

Counsellors do not diagnose mental health conditions or prescribe medication. Counselling sessions are not covered by Medicare, but you usually do not need a referral and wait times are often shorter.

## Psychologists

Psychologists are registered health professionals with university training in psychology. They can assess and diagnose mental health conditions and provide therapy for a wide range of difficulties, including more complex or long-standing ones.

With a referral and a mental health plan from your GP, you may be able to claim a Medicare rebate for a number of psychology sessions each year. There is often still a gap fee, and wait lists can be long in some areas.

## Psychiatrists

Psychiatrists are medical doctors who have specialised in mental health. They can diagnose conditions, prescribe and manage medication and provide treatment for more serious or complex mental illness. You usually need a referral from your GP to see one, and appointments are often focused on assessment and medication.

## So which one is right for me?

A few questions can help:

**Do you think you might need a diagnosis or medication?** Start with your GP. They can talk this through with you and refer you to a psychologist or psychiatrist if needed.

**Do you want someone to talk things through with, to understand what is going on and to build practical ways of coping?** Counselling may be a good place to start.

**Is cost or waiting time a barrier?** Counselling has no referral step and often shorter waits. A psychologist through Medicare may cost less per session once the rebate is applied, but you will usually need a GP appointment first.

**Are you an NDIS participant?** If you are self-managed or plan-managed and your plan has funding for therapy supports, you may be able to use it for counselling. Check your plan, or ask me on a free intro call.

These options are not either-or. Plenty of people see a counsellor while also seeing their GP or a psychiatrist. What matters most is that you feel safe, heard and comfortable with the person you are working with.
